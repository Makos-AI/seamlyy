# PART 2: GO-TO-MARKET STRATEGY MECHANICS

## CHAPTER 5: What GTM is

So far in Part 1, we spent a lot of time looking under the hood of Web Monetization. You've learned how the protocol works, how payment pointers stream micropayments, and how this flips the traditional internet business model on its head. That was the technology problem. But building the tech is only half the journey. The other half—the arguably harder half—is the people problem. 

This is where Go-To-Market (GTM) strategy comes in.

Put simply, a GTM strategy is your game plan for bringing your product to your target audience and convincing them to use it. It’s how you get your product out of your laptop and into the hands of real people. 

**THINK IT THROUGH**
Think of the last app you downloaded or service you signed up for. How did you hear about it? Why did you decide to use it? That was someone’s GTM strategy working on you.

You might be thinking, “I’m just a university student building a small side project, do I really need a whole GTM strategy?” Yes, you do. Whether you are launching a global fintech app or a small Web Monetization tool for your campus fellowship’s blog, you need a plan to reach people. Without it, you are just writing code in a vacuum. Web Monetization gives you the engine, but GTM is how you find the road and get the passengers in the car.

**KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR CHAPTER 5**
- GTM (Go-To-Market) is your plan for bringing a product to an audience.
- Web Monetization solves the technology problem of micropayments; GTM solves the people problem of distribution and adoption.
- Even small, student-led projects need a GTM strategy to find users.

## CHAPTER 6: Start with the market, not the product

Before you write a single line of code, you need to answer a fundamental question: Are you building a project, or are you building a product?

A project is built for a grade, a hackathon, or a deadline. It exists to prove you can build it. A product, on the other hand, solves a real problem for real people who will actually use it (and ideally, pay for it). Think about the OPay, Kuda, and Paystack questions. These aren't just cool engineering projects; they solve specific, painful problems for Nigerians—like failed bank transfers and the headache of online payment collection. 

**PAUSE AND PREDICT**
If you build a flawless Web Monetization tool but never talk to a single potential user, how many users do you think you'll have in a month? 

To build a product, you must start with the market. Let's look at a classic Nigerian example: Paystack.

When the founders of Paystack started out, they didn't just lock themselves in a room and code for six months. They decided to talk to business owners first. They went out and had conversations about the pain of collecting payments online in Nigeria. Their early customers—companies like Gingerbox, Jekalo, and DIYLaw—came mostly through word of mouth because the founders were actively out there talking to them. 

Interestingly, Paystack later realized that their earliest adopters were developers. Because the founders themselves were technical, they initially designed for sophisticated users. But to scale, they had to simplify the experience for non-technical business owners. 

The lesson here is simple: Start with conversations. Treat your very first users as partners and advocates. And crucially, do not assume your customers are just like you. 

**KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR CHAPTER 6**
- A project is for a grade; a product solves a real problem for real people.
- Talk to potential users before you build.
- Don't assume your users have the same technical knowledge or habits that you do.

## CHAPTER 7: The building blocks

A GTM strategy isn’t just one thing; it’s a combination of several building blocks. Let’s break them down.

### 1. Segment the market
Who exactly are you serving? You cannot say "everyone in Nigeria." That’s too broad and too expensive to reach. You need a specific segment. In our context, good examples include:
- Campus communities (e.g., Unilag engineering students)
- Small online creators (e.g., tech Twitter writers)
- WhatsApp-based vendor groups
- Informal-economy businesses

**The "Reachable Segment" Test:** Can you physically or digitally reach 50 of these people by tomorrow afternoon without spending money? If yes, it’s a reachable segment. If no, your segment is too broad or too distant.

### 2. Product category
What kind of thing is this? Is it a blog? A game? A utility tool? People need to know which mental box to put your product in.

### 3. Positioning
Why should someone choose yours over the alternatives? If you are building a monetized study guide, why is it better than just passing around a free, outdated PDF on WhatsApp? 

### 4. Value proposition
What exact value do they get? Not features, but value. "It uses Web Monetization" is a feature. "You earn ₦50 every time someone reads your notes" is a value proposition.

### 5. Distribution
How do you actually reach them? What channels will you use to get the product in front of them? 

Let’s look at M-Pesa in Kenya for a masterclass in distribution. 

M-Pesa didn't build thousands of fancy bank branches. Instead, third-party agents—the local shopkeepers selling airtime and groceries—served as their branch network. This kept infrastructure costs incredibly low. They grew their agent network at the exact pace of their customer base, ensuring that each agent had a steady volume of transactions to make it worth their while.

The lesson? Distribution can be the product. Using trusted people who already have your users' attention is a cheat code for growth. 

*Caveat:* M-Pesa started with an unusual, massive advantage: they were backed by Safaricom, the dominant telecom operator with a ready-made customer base. As a student, you don't have a telecom monopoly. You can't snap your fingers and get a million agents. But what you *can* transfer from the M-Pesa story is the idea of piggybacking on existing networks. You don't have Safaricom, but you might have the departmental WhatsApp group. Use the networks you have.

**TRY IT**
Pick an idea you have for a Web Monetization product. Run it through the "Reachable Segment" test. Can you find 50 potential users by tomorrow? Where are they?

**KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR CHAPTER 7**
- Segment your market to a specific, reachable group.
- Focus on the value proposition (what they get), not just features (how it works).
- Distribution can be your biggest advantage; leverage existing networks and trusted intermediaries.

## CHAPTER 8: The three GTM motions

How exactly do you drive adoption? There are three main "motions" or engines for growth.

1. **Product-led:** The product sells itself through the user experience. People try it, get instant value, love it, and tell their friends. (Think of how you likely discovered WhatsApp or Telegram).
2. **Sales-led:** You personally sell, demo, and onboard users one by one. You are the engine. 
3. **Market-led:** Content, community, and partnerships drive awareness. You write blog posts, host Twitter Spaces, or partner with student associations.

Which one fits a student trying to get their first 0 to 100 users? 

The reality is, it's usually a mix. When you are going from 0 to 10 users, it is deeply **sales-led**. You are literally standing next to your roommate, showing them how to install a Web Monetization wallet, and making sure it works. 

From 10 to 50 users, you might shift to **product-led** (your roommate tells their classmates because the tool is genuinely useful) combined with **market-led** (you post a thread on Twitter/X or share a demo video in a campus Telegram group). 

Don't worry about automating everything on day one. Do things that don't scale.

**KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR CHAPTER 8**
- Product-led, Sales-led, and Market-led are the three engines of growth.
- For your first 100 users, expect to do a lot of manual, unscalable sales-led work to get your first 10 users onboarded.

## CHAPTER 9: The funnel (the longest chapter)

GTM is often visualized as a funnel. At the top, you have a lot of people who hear about you. In the middle, fewer people actually try the product. At the bottom, even fewer become regular, retained users. Let's break down the funnel for the Nigerian context.

### 1. Acquire
This is how you find and reach your first people. For a student in Nigeria, you aren't running expensive Google Ads. You rely on:
- **WhatsApp groups:** The lifeblood of Nigerian communication. Departmental groups, faculty groups, fellowship groups. 
- **Campus communities:** Tech clubs (like GDSC or unilorin tech community), faculty associations.
- **Twitter/X threads & Instagram:** Going where people already spend their time.
- **Word of mouth and Referrals:** The most powerful tool you have.

**TRY IT: Acquisition**
Write a 2-line message you would send to your class WhatsApp group to get them to try your Web Monetization project. Make sure it highlights the value, not the tech.

### 2. Activate
Acquisition gets them to the door; activation gets them inside and experiencing value. Activation is defining and shortening the path to first value. 

In Nigeria, you have unique hurdles: data and airtime cost sensitivity, and low trust for new digital tools (the "is this a scam?" factor). You have to do a lot of hand-holding. 

For Web Monetization specifically, activation has a very clear definition: **The first streamed payment.**
But wait! The visitor must have a compatible wallet to stream a payment. Therefore, for your product, activation *must* include onboarding the user to a Web Monetization wallet. If they don't set up the wallet, they can't experience the product. 

### 3. Retain
Retention means giving people a reason to return. It's about building habit, providing great support, and creating feedback loops. 
For a Web Monetized product, retention is defined as **continued flow**. Are they coming back to stream micropayments again next week? If not, you have a leaky bucket. 

**THINK IT THROUGH**
If it takes 15 minutes and 50MB of data to set up a Web Monetization wallet before a user can even read your content, will they stick around? How can you shorten this path to first value?

### Measuring a 100-user funnel by hand
You do not need fancy analytics software (like Mixpanel or Google Analytics) for your first 100 users. A spreadsheet or a physical notebook works perfectly. 
- Column A: Name (Acquired)
- Column B: Wallet Set Up? (Activated part 1)
- Column C: First Payment Streamed? (Activated part 2)
- Column D: Returned in Week 2? (Retained)

Let's look at Chowdeck, the Nigerian food delivery giant, when they were just starting.
Before launching officially in October 2021, the founders tested the idea with just a few bikes and two partner restaurants. In their very first month, they had only 319 users. But here is the critical part: they kept unit economics positive from the outset. They didn't heavily subsidize orders just to show fake growth. They specifically aimed at customers who valued time over price.

The lesson? Start tiny, do it by hand, pick a segment that values what you offer, and don't buy growth with losses. 

**KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR CHAPTER 9**
- The GTM funnel is Acquire, Activate, Retain.
- In Web Monetization, Activation = getting a wallet set up + the first streamed payment.
- Retention = continued flow of micropayments.
- Track your first 100 users manually in a spreadsheet.

## CHAPTER 10: Your first 100 users

Let's bring it all together. Connecting your GTM strategy back to the Web Monetization protocol means realizing that your early users need to be nurtured. 

A good strategy for your first 100 users might be finding a small, tight-knit community (like a campus study group) that you can onboard by hand. You sit with them, help them set up their wallets, explain how the micropayments work, and get them to experience that magic moment of the first streamed payment. 

Once they are activated, you focus on retention: keeping the content or tool so useful that they continue to return, ensuring continued flow. 

Here is your master worksheet for getting those first 100 users.

**TRY IT: The First-100 Worksheet**
Grab a pen and paper, or open a new note, and fill this in for your project:

1. **The Segment:** Who are the specific 100 people I want to reach? (e.g., 300-level accounting students).
2. **The Acquisition Channel:** Exactly where will I find them? (e.g., The PHY301 tutorial WhatsApp group).
3. **The Hook:** What is the 2-sentence pitch that focuses on their pain point?
4. **The Activation Plan:** How will I get them to set up a wallet? Will I make a 60-second tutorial video, or do it in person?
5. **The Retention Hook:** Why will they come back on Day 2 or Week 2? 

Fill this out. Don't overthink it. The goal is to move from theory to action.

## KEY TAKEAWAYS FOR PART 2

- **Market First:** Start with the market, not the product. Talk to users before building.
- **Segmenting in Nigeria:** Use campus communities, small online creators, WhatsApp-based groups, informal-economy businesses. Always apply the "what counts as a reachable segment" test.
- **The Funnel:** 
  - *Acquire* covers word of mouth, communities, and referrals. 
  - *Activate* covers hand-holding, data/airtime cost sensitivity, and trust. For Web Monetization, the visitor must have a compatible wallet, so activation includes onboarding and the first streamed payment.
  - *Retain* covers habit, support, and feedback loops, defined as continued flow.
- **Manual Work:** For your first 100 users, do things that don't scale. Use spreadsheets, onboard people in person, and leverage the networks you already have.
